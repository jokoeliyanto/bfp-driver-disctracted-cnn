Background

Road crashes kill about 1.19 million people a year (WHO, 2023). Distraction is one of the few causes that sits entirely inside the car: in the United States alone, distraction-affected crashes killed 3,275 people in 2023 (NHTSA).

Our team started from the same worry in Indonesia, where accident numbers kept climbing and many crashes come down to drivers who were not watching the road. Phones are the main culprit, yet every driver already carries one, which makes the phone itself a natural place for a safety assistant.

The problem we set out to solve

Detect distracted driving in real time, on a phone, without an internet connection.

  • Classify the driver into one of ten activities from a single camera frame.
  • Turn that into a yes/no decision, distracted or focused, and alert the driver immediately.
  • Fit on a mid-range Android phone: small model, low latency, no server round-trip.

The ten activities and the 22,424 labelled photos come from the State Farm Distracted Driver Detection challenge on Kaggle, posted by a US car insurer for the same reason.

Who would pay for this, and why

Ride-hailing & delivery

Platforms with thousands of drivers on the road all day want fewer crashes, fewer insurance claims and safer trips for passengers. An in-app alert costs almost nothing per driver.

Logistics & bus fleets

Fleet managers need evidence of risky behaviour for coaching, not just after-the-fact crash reports. Aggregated alert counts per trip give them a leading indicator.

Insurers

Usage-based insurance prices risk from behaviour. Distraction events per 100 km are a stronger signal than age or postcode, and could reward attentive drivers with lower premiums.

Why MobileNet

Our first model, a CNN trained from scratch, actually scored slightly higher on validation ({{ baseline.best_val_accuracy|pct:1 }} vs {{ deployed.best_val_accuracy|pct:1 }}), but it took {{ baseline.seconds_per_epoch }} s per epoch to train and learned everything from 26 drivers. We chose transfer learning from ImageNet MobileNet because:

  • Built for phones: depthwise-separable convolutions keep inference light enough for real time.
  • Less room to memorise: the frozen ImageNet features cannot adapt to the 26 drivers in the dataset, so the model has to rely on general visual cues. (Whether that helps on new drivers still needs a driver-split test.)
  • Faster to iterate: {{ deployed.seconds_per_epoch }} s per epoch instead of {{ baseline.seconds_per_epoch }} s, with dropout against overfitting.

Risks and what we would do next

  • Right-hand-drive cars. State Farm photos come from US cars; in Indonesia the driver sits on the right. Mirroring the images (and swapping the left/right labels) or collecting local data is needed before launch.
  • New drivers. Re-evaluate with a split by driver, so the score reflects people the model has never seen.
  • Night and cabin variety. Add infrared frames, different car interiors and camera positions.
  • Temporal smoothing. Alert only after the distraction lasts a few frames, to cut false alarms.

Privacy by design

Pointing a camera at a person needs care. The design keeps video on the phone: frames are classified on the device and only the label, never the image, would leave it. Fleet use would require the driver's informed consent and clear rules about who sees the alert log. This web demo classifies uploads in memory and does not store them.
